Hold your left and right hands out in front of you, palms facing down. Both hands share the exact same components: four fingers, one thumb, identical joints, and matching proportions. Yet, no matter how you twist, rotate, or turn them in three-dimensional space, you can never slide your left hand directly on top of your right hand so that both thumbs and palms align.

They are mirror images that cannot be superimposed.

In chemistry, molecules exhibit this exact same geometric property, known as **chirality**—derived from the Greek word *kheir*, meaning "hand." Many organic molecules can assemble into two distinct three-dimensional versions called **enantiomers**: a left-handed form (designated **L**) and a right-handed form (designated **D**).

In an ordinary laboratory beaker, when chemists synthesize chiral molecules from scratch, nature plays completely fair. Because both mirror forms possess identical atomic bonds, identical weights, and identical thermodynamic stability, chemical reactions always produce an exact **50:50 racemic mixture**—half left-handed, half right-handed.

Yet, when you peer inside any living cell on Earth—from a deep-sea hydrothermal bacterium to a redwood tree or a human neuron—that symmetry vanishes completely.

Every protein in your body is constructed exclusively from **left-handed (L) amino acids**. Conversely, the structural backbones of your DNA and RNA are built exclusively from **right-handed (D) sugars**. 

This universal single-handedness of biology is called **homochirality**, and explaining how lifeless chemistry broke its fifty-fifty symmetry remains one of the deepest unsolved mysteries in the origin of life.

## Pasteur and the Micro-Crystals of Wine

The discovery of molecular handedness began in 1848 with a 25-year-old French chemist named Louis Pasteur.

Pasteur was examining salts of tartaric acid—a crystalline byproduct deposited inside wine barrels during fermentation. Chemists had long been baffled by a strange discrepancy: tartaric acid harvested from living grapes rotated a beam of polarized light as it passed through solution, whereas chemically identical tartaric acid synthesized in a factory left polarized light completely unaffected.

Looking closely at the synthetic tartaric acid crystals under a microscope, Pasteur noticed something everyone else had missed:

* The synthetic batch actually contained **two subtly different crystal shapes**—tiny asymmetric facets that were exact mirror images of one another.
* Using a pair of fine tweezers, Pasteur painstakingly separated the left-facing crystals from the right-facing crystals into two separate piles.
* When he dissolved the left-facing crystals in water, they rotated polarized light to the left. When he dissolved the right-facing crystals, they rotated light to the exact same angle to the right.
* When mixed back together in equal parts, their opposing rotations canceled each other out completely.

Pasteur had uncovered the three-dimensional geometry of molecules—and realized immediately that asymmetric handedness was a fundamental dividing line between inert chemistry and living organisms.
